# AI and Honest Disclosure

[AI Policy for Professor Bear's Courses | Using AI Responsibly in Class](https://youtu.be/8Ut0Cdl6vMw?si=9w3aEpt1ZyAR4Kiz)

**What this page is.** How the course [AI policy](../prerequisites/ai-policy.md) meets the Frictional log. The policy is canonical; this page only explains how to satisfy it in your entries.

**The short version.** AI assistance is allowed and expected — this course assumes Claude Code. Log it. A log showing heavy, well-bounded AI use can score 10/10. Undisclosed use is the problem; use is not.

---

## What the policy already requires

From [prerequisites/ai-policy.md](../prerequisites/ai-policy.md), in its own terms:

- Credit starting repositories, source passages, collaborators, and AI assistance.
- Identify what you contributed and what Claude contributed.
- A generated first draft is a starting point for inspection and improvement.
- The instructor or TA may ask you to explain code, methods, results, and decisions.
- Preserve original predictions and evidence; distinguish actual executions, simulations, fixtures, and unsupported claims.
- **AI may organize your authentic effort notes; it must not invent struggle, timestamps, understanding, or human approvals.**
- AI use alone is not misconduct. Contribution, understanding, and evidence remain your responsibility.

The Frictional log is where most of that gets recorded. CONTRIBUTIONS.md carries the summary; the log carries the per-session detail.

## Why the policy is permissive

Two reasons, and neither is indifference to learning.

A restrictive rule that cannot be enforced is worse than a permissive one that can. Detection does not work and works less well each year, and a rule enforceable only against the honest is a tax on honesty.

More importantly: **the tool is not the problem — invisible cognition is.** A student who asks Claude a hard conceptual question, gets a real answer, tries to apply it, fails, works out why it failed, and records that has done substantial cognitive work. A student who pastes a prompt and ships the output has done none. Both used the same tool. The log is what distinguishes them, and it is the only thing that can.

## What good AI use looks like in a log

The pattern that scores 2 on the contributions row has four parts: **you tried first, you asked something specific, you tested what came back, and you can say what you did with it.**

> *Spent 20 minutes trying to see why temperature=0 still gave me two different outputs, couldn't. Asked Claude what makes greedy decoding non-deterministic — it gave three causes and the one about tie-breaking across equal logits matched what I was seeing. Rejected its proposed fix (seed everything and call it deterministic) because that hides the property instead of documenting it; recorded the observed variation in the conclusion as a limit on repeatability. Claude found the cause; the decision to report it rather than suppress it is mine.*

Everything the row asks for is there: the attempt that preceded the question, the specificity of the question, the verification, and the disposition — accepted, changed, rejected.

Compare:

> *Asked Claude to write the function. It worked.*

This is honestly disclosed and it is **not misconduct**. It is also a 1 at best on the contributions row and supplies nothing to the friction or learning rows. Honest disclosure of doing nothing is still a record of doing nothing.

## When the artifact is mostly not yours

This happens and is worth saying plainly rather than leaving you to guess.

If your log is honest and shows the substance was generated rather than developed, you have followed the policy. What you have submitted is an artifact with little evidence of your learning attached. Frictional scores near the floor, the implementation rubric may not be satisfied either, and the instructor or TA may ask you to explain the code — which is a standing provision of the policy, not a special measure.

**The consequence is academic, not disciplinary.** That outcome is far preferable to an undisclosed version of the same thing, because an honest record is something that can be addressed in a conversation in October rather than a proceeding in December.

## When use is undisclosed

That is an academic integrity matter handled through the university's normal process.

Fabricating a process record — inventing struggles, dating entries to work sessions that did not happen, generating a log to accompany generated work — is treated more seriously than undisclosed use alone. Falsifying evidence is a different and worse act than omitting it, and separating repeatability from correctness is the subject matter of this course.

Be clear-eyed about the incentives, which are deliberate: **the honest path is also the cheaper path.** Documenting real work takes about twelve minutes. Manufacturing a convincing false record requires inventing specific confusions, plausible dead ends, a coherent trajectory across four sessions, commits that corroborate it, and an uncertainty that develops — which is most of the cognitive work of the Assignment, performed badly and under worse conditions. If you find yourself doing that, stop and do the Assignment. It is genuinely less work. That is not a slogan; it is the design principle the whole framework rests on.

## Help from people

Identical rules. Study groups, the TA, a classmate, a friend in industry — all permitted, all logged the same way, all with what you accepted, changed, or rejected.

Collaboration is not a lesser form of learning and the contributions record is not a confession. It is the same instrument as an acknowledgments section, a methods section, and a citation list. Keeping one is a professional skill, and this course happens to grade it.

## What the course will not do

Your submissions will not be run through an AI detector. A well-written artifact will not be treated as suspicious on that basis. A log will not lose points for being messy, blunt, or fragmentary.

And if something in your log does not add up, you will be asked about it before anything else happens.
